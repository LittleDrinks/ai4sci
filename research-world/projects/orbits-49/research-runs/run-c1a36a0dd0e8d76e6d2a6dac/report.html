Stability of Planetary Orbits: Why Planets Do Not Decay Into Collisions or the Sun

The premise that planetary orbits gradually decay and will eventually cause planets to swirl into the Sun is false. Over gigayear timescales comparable to the lifetime of the Solar System, planetary orbits are effectively stable. The semi-major axes of the planets undergo only small oscillations with no secular (long-term cumulative) drift. While the inner Solar System is chaotic—meaning its precise state becomes unpredictable beyond tens of millions of years—the chaotic variations are tightly constrained by quasiconserved quantities and angular momentum conservation, so catastrophic collisions or orbital collapse remain exceedingly rare events. Numerical integrations confirm that approximately 99% of plausible future trajectories keep all planets in stable, non-colliding orbits for at least 5 billion years.

Secular Theory: No Orbital Decay at Lowest Orders

The question of whether planetary orbits drift apart or converge was central to the development of celestial mechanics. In the late 18th century, Lagrange and Laplace formulated the equations of motion for planetary perturbations under Newton's law of universal gravitation. Their analysis showed that, to first order in the planetary masses, the semi-major axes of the planets undergo only small periodic oscillations and possess no secular terms—that is, there is no long-term cumulative increase or decrease in orbital size [Laskar, Scholarpedia, lines 107–124]. Poisson, Haretu, and Poincaré later demonstrated that this result persists through second order in the planetary masses, reinforcing the conclusion that orbital decay is not a generic feature of gravitational N-body dynamics.

Laplace also identified a quasi-resonant term involving Jupiter and Saturn (proportional to $2\lambda_{\mathrm{Jupiter}} - 5\lambda_{\mathrm{Saturn}}$) with an amplitude of $46'50''$ and a period of approximately 900 years, which explained apparent discrepancies in historical observations without requiring any secular drift in the planets' distances from the Sun [Laskar, Scholarpedia, lines 125–135].

The eccentricities and inclinations of planetary orbits are similarly constrained. Laplace showed that, provided all planets orbit in the same direction, polynomial or exponential solutions cannot exist for the linearized system of eccentricity and inclination evolution. The resulting solutions are quasiperiodic combinations of circular motions with well-defined frequencies—the secular frequencies of the Solar System—producing only bounded oscillations in orbital shape and orientation [Laskar, Scholarpedia, lines 150–180].

Chaos Without Catastrophe

Modern numerical integrations have revealed that the Solar System is chaotic, with a Lyapunov time of approximately 5 million years [Laskar, Scholarpedia, lines 200–210; Mogavero et al., Phys. Rev. X 13, 021018]. This means that two trajectories starting with slightly different initial conditions diverge exponentially, with an error of just 15 metres in Earth's present position growing to about 150 million kilometres after 100 million years. Consequently, precise prediction of individual planetary positions beyond roughly 60 million years is impossible.

However, chaos does not imply instability or orbital decay. Chaos describes sensitivity to initial conditions, not divergence of orbital parameters to infinity. Over 25 billion years of numerical integration, the maximum eccentricity reached by the outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) remains almost constant, reflecting their near-regular, quasiperiodic trajectories. Venus and Earth exhibit moderated but significant variations, with Earth's eccentricity reaching approximately 0.08 (compared to its current range of ~0.06) and Venus showing similar bounds. Mars can reach eccentricities up to ~0.2, and Mercury's eccentricity can occasionally exceed 0.5 through chaotic diffusion [Laskar, Scholarpedia, lines 279–288].

Critically, these large excursions remain bounded. Angular momentum conservation strongly constrains the most massive planets, and the system as a whole never enters a regime of runaway orbital decay. The Solar System is in a state of marginal stability: it is technically unstable in principle, but catastrophic phenomena capable of destroying its current architecture require timescales comparable to the age of the Solar System itself (~5 billion years) [Laskar, Scholarpedia, lines 310–340].

Quasi-Integrals of Motion and Timescale Separation

A deeper understanding of why the Solar System remains stable despite chaos comes from recent work by Mogavero, Hoang, and Laskar (2023), who computed the full Lyapunov spectrum of a forced secular model of the inner planets [Mogavero et al., Phys. Rev. X 13, 021018]. They uncovered a hierarchy of characteristic exponents spanning two orders of magnitude, revealing a slow-fast dynamical structure with fundamental timescales ranging from 5 to 500 million years.

Their analysis of the Hamiltonian's Fourier harmonics identified three symmetries characterizing the strongest resonances responsible for orbital chaos. These symmetries are broken only by weak resonances, leading to the existence of quasi-integrals of motion—quantities that are nearly conserved over gigayear timescales. A principal component analysis of orbital solutions independently confirmed that these quasi-integrals correspond to the slowest degrees of freedom of the dynamics. The statistical stability of the inner planets over the Solar System's lifetime emerges naturally from the adiabatic constraints exerted by these slowly varying quasiconserved quantities [Mogavero et al., Phys. Rev. X 13, 021018, abstract and popular summary].

This framework explains how the Solar System can be simultaneously chaotic (unpredictable beyond ~60 Myr) and statistically stable (collision-free for ~5 Gyr in most trajectories). The quasi-integrals act as effective barriers to chaotic diffusion, preventing orbital parameters from exploring the full range of phase space.

The Stabilizing Role of General Relativity

General relativity plays a crucial role in maintaining the long-term stability of the inner Solar System. General relativity increases the perihelion precession rate of Mercury by 0.43 arcseconds per year, shifting it from 5.15 arcsec/yr to 5.58 arcsec/yr. This shift moves Mercury's precession frequency further from that of Jupiter (4.25 arcsec/yr), thereby detuning the dangerous Mercury–Jupiter perihelion resonance that would otherwise facilitate large eccentricity growth [Laskar, Scholarpedia, lines 420–442].

The quantitative impact of this effect is dramatic. In 2501 numerical solutions of the full Solar System integrated over 5 billion years using a realistic model including general relativity, approximately 99% of trajectories evolve without any collisions or ejections. In roughly 1% of cases, Mercury's eccentricity increases sufficiently to cause a collision with Venus or the Sun within 5 Gyr. However, in a pure Newtonian model (without general relativity) initialized with the same present-day conditions, the probability of collisions within 5 Gyr rises to approximately 60% [Laskar & Gastineau, Nature 459, 817–819 (2009), as cited in Laskar, Scholarpedia, lines 420–442]. This provides compelling indirect confirmation of general relativity's contribution to Solar System stability.

Collision Scenarios: Rare Events, Not Inevitable Fate

The most extreme outcomes predicted by numerical simulations involve collisions between terrestrial planets. In one trajectory out of the 2501 studied, the eccentricity increase of Mercury is followed by a cascade of destabilization affecting Mars and the entire inner Solar System, leading to collisions between planets or between a planet and the Sun within 100 million years of the cascade onset (at approximately 3.4 Gyr from the present). Specific outcomes in this destabilized region include one Mercury–Earth collision, 29 Mars–Earth collisions, and 18 Venus–Earth collisions [Laskar & Gastineau, Nature 459, 817–819 (2009), as cited in Laskar, Scholarpedia, lines 420–442].

These scenarios are important because they demonstrate that the Solar System is not perfectly stable in an absolute sense. However, their rarity—occurring in approximately 1% of equiprobable trajectories consistent with current observational uncertainties—underscores that orbital decay is not the default outcome. The vast majority of possible futures preserve the current architecture of the Solar System until the Sun evolves into a red giant, which will fundamentally alter the system on a timescale of approximately 5 billion years regardless of gravitational dynamics.

Conclusion

Planetary orbits do not decay and planets will not swirl into the Sun. The foundation of this stability lies in the structure of Newtonian gravity combined with the constraints of angular momentum conservation: semi-major axes undergo only bounded oscillations, while eccentricities and inclinations are limited by quasiperiodic coupling and, more recently understood, by quasi-integrals of motion that constrain chaotic diffusion. General relativity provides an additional stabilizing mechanism by detuning key resonances involving Mercury. While the Solar System is chaotic and technically unstable—with a collision probability of approximately 1% over the remaining 5-billion-year lifetime of the Sun—this represents a tiny minority of possible futures. The overwhelming majority of trajectories maintain stable, non-colliding planetary orbits throughout the Sun's main-sequence lifetime, confirming that the Solar System is in a state of marginal stability consistent with its age.
